What the track record does not show

  • Four cases are an illustration, not a validation [Certain]. The cases, corridors and codes come from the same public reporting, so the test is not blind [Likely].
  • The extra case and the two extra hits with the monthly check are US-02's, and that gain was foreseen: run 1's report pointed to Armenia's monthly data before run 2 was designed. It shows the mechanism works; it is not fresh evidence [Certain]. The monthly check also moved EU-05's first lead earlier, from 12 to 17 months before the action [Certain].
  • Of the hits shown, two had stopped being leads before the action, by 5 and 9 months [Certain].
  • Tests whose UK flow is under the display rule are listed with no route and no result, whatever the result was; the counts above cover the tests shown, and the research log in the repository keeps every result [Certain].
  • The figures are today's, after revisions, not what the radar would have seen at the time [Certain].
  • Integrated circuits rise on many corridors at once: the same codes were a lead at some run before the US-01 and EU-05 dates on 4 of the 8 other corridors in the backtest, so a lead on them says little about one route [Certain].
  • The backtest covers the nine corridors of runs 1 and 2. Run 3 added nine corridors to the live radar on the same rule and did not rerun or extend the backtest, so the track record says nothing about them [Certain].
  • The European dates are known only to the month: EU-05's charges as reported, and the earliest dated step found for EU-01. EU-01 had earlier public steps that the sources do not date, so its lead times may be too long [Likely]; for EU-05 an earlier step is possible [Guessing]. Dating both to the first of the month can make lead times, and how long before the action a hit had stopped, up to a month too short [Certain].
  • Codes are mapped from the cases' descriptions of the goods, not stated in them. EU-01 concerned mainly computers, outside the radar's codes, so its tests check the route on a weak match; US-02's avionics could fall under several codes [Certain].
  • The peacetime years held the pandemic's swings in trade, and sanctions on Russia from 2014 were already in force. The peacetime count used the annual check alone and is an indication, not a measured false alarm rate [Certain].

The rules

  • UK signal. UK exports to the corridor over the trailing 12 published months at least 2x the 2019-21 annual average and at least £20,000 above it, for 3 published months running. HMRC month M counts from the end of month M + 2.
  • Onward signal. The corridor's own exports of the code to Russia: the latest year released (2022 or later), or, for the … of the … corridors with monthly UN Comtrade data after 2019, the 12 months to the latest month released. At least 2x the 2019-21 level and at least $100,000.
  • Lead. Both signals at one monthly run. A UK signal alone is a watch item.
  • Release dates. Each year or month of onward data counts only from the release date UN Comtrade records for it; where a dataset was replaced, that may be the replacement's date, so a signal can come late but not early [Likely]. The exception is the 2019-21 baselines, used at every run though some were released later [Certain].
  • The rules were fixed and committed before each backtest ran [Certain].

The display rule

A flagged series is shown only when UK exports over the 12 published months reach £… and came in at least … separate months, and a past run is drawn only when its own 12 months pass the same test. A series or run whose 12 months are smaller is counted, never shown by corridor or code, and a backtest test whose UK flow is smaller is listed with no route and no result, whatever the result was. The 2019-21 average is shown however small, as pre-war context. This makes it less likely that a small flow points at one exporter; it cannot rule that out [Likely]. The months test screens out flows that came in fewer than 6 months, but one large consignment with small sales in other months can still pass [Certain]. The levels are judgement calls, not taken from HMRC's disclosure rules. On a corridor and goods heading that appear in the enforcement cases, only 12-month windows that start after the cases' first public actions are drawn.

Limits of a lead

  • Ordinary trade can pass both tests: a corridor's own demand can rise with its exports to Russia [Certain].
  • A lead can rest on onward trade that has since stopped. The United Arab Emirates and Tajikistan have released no annual data for years after 2023; the UAE has no monthly data after 2019 and Tajikistan none, so their onward check rests on 2023, as both UAE leads shown do [Certain]. Vietnam's annual and monthly data also end in 2023, and China's monthly data end in December 2024 [Certain].
  • A 2019-21 year counts in a corridor's annual onward baseline only when its figures hold exports of one of the nine codes to Russia that year. Macao's hold none for 2019-21, so its annual check cannot pass, and its monthly baseline is zero, so its monthly check rests on the $100,000 floor alone. Azerbaijan's hold none for 2019 and Georgia's none for 2020, so their baselines are the average of the other two years [Certain].
  • Hong Kong's reported exports include re-exports, so its onward figures also count goods that only passed through it [Likely].
  • One month can carry the monthly onward check. The rule itself does not test how spread out the exports are on either side; only the display rule's months test does, on the UK side [Certain].
  • The codes are broad, and most of what they cover is in ordinary use [Certain].
  • The onward check rests on what each corridor reports to UN Comtrade as its exports to Russia [Certain].
  • Figures are revised after publication [Certain].

What it does

Each month, once HMRC publishes its trade figures, the radar checks every pairing of a corridor country and a goods code from the high-priority list. A lead is a pairing where UK exports to the corridor rose well above their 2019-21 level and the corridor’s own exports of the same goods to Russia rose too; a UK rise alone is a watch item. A rise in exports is a lead, not evidence of a breach: a question for an analyst to check.

Who uses it

  • A team lead reads this dashboard: how many leads this run made, which are shown, and how the method did against published enforcement cases.
  • An analyst works each lead in the analyst workspace: its figures, a brief, the checklist from official guidance.
  • A second person approves each brief there, returns it or rejects it. With four-eyes on, the default, nobody approves their own.

How to read the dashboard

  1. Now: the run’s four figures, then each lead and watch item shown, with the UK figures and the onward check behind it. Violet marks a lead.
  2. Track record: the backtest, case by case, against published enforcement cases, by official reference only.
  3. Method and sources: the rules, the display rule that holds small flows back, and where each figure comes from.

What is demo data

The figures here are published aggregates: HMRC overseas trade statistics and UN Comtrade. The workspace adds a synthetic layer (declarations, an exporter’s shipments, reports), made up from a seed so it can be shared.

What a deployment could add

  • Data on release day: the run on a schedule when HMRC and UN Comtrade publish, rather than when someone starts it.
  • Sign-in and roles, with an audit log the database writes. A hosted version is built and tested, not deployed.
  • The owner’s own rules: the display threshold, the corridors and goods codes, the checklist.

What a lead is worth

A lead is a question, ranked: two published series rose together on one route, so it is worth an analyst's look before anything else. It is not evidence of a breach, not a finding and never a named exporter. Its worth is the order it puts the checks in, not a forecast of outcomes.

The analyst workspace turns this into hours: enter how long a brief takes and it works out what a run would take, on your figures only.

Improving it

This is demo data and a first set of rules, and both can be improved further: more corridors and goods codes, monthly onward data where publishers release it, and a longer backtest as more cases are published, as four cases is a small test.
